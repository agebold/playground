# Design Principles — Weight Management App

Distilled from the research. These are the rules of thumb every design decision should pass through. If a choice violates one of these, flag it.

---

## 1. Guidance, not tracking

Older adults in our audience want to be *told what to do*, not asked to *record what they did*. Meal plans, shopping lists, exercise routines = yes. Food logs, habit checkboxes, sleep diaries = no. Photo capture is acceptable only as a temporary onboarding tool, never as the long-term interaction. *(Source: [[findings]] #9–12.)*

## 2. Help before the decision, not after it

Be useful at the moment of choice — what to order at the restaurant, what to put in the cart, which exercise to do today. Logging after the fact is the wrong moment. *(Source: [[findings]] #10.)*

## 3. Lose fat, not strength — frame everything in body composition, not scale weight

Avoid % body weight loss as a headline. Lead with the *kind* of weight: lean muscle mass preserved, fat reduced, bone density protected. *(Source: [[findings]] #6, #17–18.)*

## 4. Pill-first; never make a needle the hero

The pill option is the default visual and verbal frame. The pen is a secondary option for people who prefer it. Never show a vial or syringe as the hero image. Never lead with compounded medication. *(Source: [[findings]] #2; anti-patterns.)*

## 5. Trust signals must be paired

"FDA-approved" alone triggers cost/coverage questions. Always pair safety signals (FDA, name brand, provider oversight) with affordability + coverage signals so users don't bounce on the price question. *(Source: [[findings]] #4.)*

## 6. Provider as safety net AND motivational accountability — never as chatty coach

A real provider in the loop is reassuring in two ways: (a) clinically, for side effects, dosage, and safety, and (b) emotionally, as someone who *notices* and helps users keep going ("I'm not in it by myself"). Lead with the motivational outcome — "someone who keeps you on track" — and let the credential follow. A nutritionist or coach role may carry the motivational load as well as a physician does. What is NOT reassuring is chatty messaging that tells users things they already know. Keep provider touchpoints sparse, purposeful, and tied to either medication safety or motivational accountability. *(Source: [[findings]] #5, #27.)*

## 7. Older-adult-specific by default — no generic fitness aesthetics

Every exercise image, every routine, every illustration must read as accessible to someone 65+. Seated options, balance-safe routines, joint-friendly movements, no equipment beyond what's already at home. If a screen could be mistaken for a generic fitness app, redo it. Standing tolerance can be under 5 minutes — design for that. *(Source: [[findings]] #15–16.)*

## 8. No wearables, no extra devices

Don't design around step counts, continuous sleep data, or anything that implies the user must buy or wear something new. Even references to wearables on otherwise-good screens caused rejection. *(Source: [[findings]] #12.)*

## 9. Non-restrictive eating frame

Many users have restrictive-diet fatigue (keto, low-sugar for pre-diabetic/diabetic). Position diet as *adding* — protein, strength foods, bone-supporting nutrients — not *removing*. *(Source: [[findings]] #13.)*

## 10. Convenience for how this audience actually eats

Many users are takeout/delivery/prepared-food heavy and cook less. Meet them there. Don't assume meal-prep workflows. Restaurant and delivery guidance is a feature, not an edge case. *(Source: [[findings]] #14.)*

## 11. Cost is a feature, not a footnote

$50/month vs the $300–700 anchor is itself a value proposition. Surface it early and clearly. Tie it to Medicare coverage. *(Source: [[findings]] #19–20; [[decisions]] 2026-05-05.)*

## 12. Body composition tracking must feel light

Users want lean mass / bone density / fat-vs-muscle outcomes — but immediately ask "how does that work, do I need a device, do I need a lab?" Whatever the measurement strategy ends up being (DEXA, smart scale, photo, lab), the UX must make it feel low-effort and low-cost. If it feels burdensome, the value prop collapses. *(Source: [[findings]] #6, #28; [[open-questions]].)*

## 13. "Strength, not restriction" is the lead headline phrase

Across all 4 bullseye participants this was the single most resonant phrase, regardless of which concept they preferred. Use it as the program's anchor copy. Carry it across all surfaces — marketing, onboarding, in-app — not just one concept's hero. *(Source: [[findings]] #25.)*

## 14. Coverage check is a CTA, not a footer

"Check My Coverage" was both a trust anchor and a conversion trigger in research — 4/4 reacted, 2 said they'd switch plans for it. Always surface coverage status (or the ability to check it) early and prominently on any acquisition surface. Prefer an interactive moment to a passive link. *(Source: [[findings]] #26.)*

## 15. Trust = specific numbers, recognizable names, no logo soup

On any acquisition surface, one strong trust hook beats five generic ones. Concrete consumer numbers ("over 100,000 members," app-store ratings) outperform a press-logo wall. When a press logo *is* used, prefer a single consumer-recognizable anchor (NYT) over a row of mixed B2B logos — Med City News and TechCrunch read as noise to a 65+ audience. On provider cards, lead with "Dr." / "NP" prefixes and a minute-count for appointment length so credibility signals can't be mistaken for age. *(Source: [[findings]] #33, #37–39; 2026-05-21 LP review.)*
