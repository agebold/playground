# Winning Value Propositions

The six value props named in the 2026-05-05 readout as what the program should be built around. Listed in priority order. Each one is anchored to the research that validated it.

---

## 1. FDA-approved GLP-1 options that fit your life, covered by Medicare

The first decision gate. Users do not evaluate the rest of the program until they know whether the medication feels safe, affordable, and tolerable.

- **What resonates:** pill-first (with pen as backup), FDA approval, clear coverage / affordability, provider oversight, side-effect management.
- **What to avoid:** vial/needle as hero, leading with compounded medication, "GLP-1 access" without immediately clarifying cost, coverage, safety, oversight.
- **User need (verbatim):** *"Help me access a GLP-1 that's safe, affordable, and that I can actually take."*

*(Source: 2026-05-05 readout, "Value Prop 1: GLP-1: Safety, Form Factor & Coverage." [[findings]] #1–5.)*

## 2. Lose fat, not strength (marketing message)

The core differentiator. Connects GLP-1s, aging, muscle, body composition, safety, and exercise in one promise. This is the headline tagline for the program.

- **Why it wins:** Competing GLP-1 brands promise % body weight loss. Bold's wedge is the *kind* of weight lost — protect the muscle and bone that matter as you age.
- **Companion headline phrase:** *"Strength, not restriction."* 4/4 bullseye participants reacted positively to this phrase across all concepts — it should anchor copy alongside "Lose fat, not strength" across marketing, onboarding, and in-app. *(See [[findings]] #25; [[principles]] #13.)*
- **Headline anti-pattern:** *"Lose weight by going beyond the scale"* tested as too generic for the lab-depth content it framed (Robin called this out directly). If we keep any lab-depth angle in product or marketing copy, the headline must communicate clinical depth more concretely. *(See [[findings]] #30.)*

*(Source: 2026-05-05 readout, "Winning value props" #2. [[findings]] #17–18, #25, #30.)*

## 3. Body composition tracking made easy

The strongest differentiator after GLP-1 trust. Users want proof they're losing fat without losing strength, muscle, or bone.

- **What resonates:** lean mass tracking, fat loss vs muscle loss, body composition over scale weight, bone density / osteoporosis cues, "is this healthy weight loss?"
- **What to avoid:** over-indexing on a "metabolic health score," labs that feel like generic PCP bloodwork, showing body comp tracking without explaining how it works, assuming people will accept extra devices or special labs without a clear benefit.
- **User need (verbatim):** *"Show me that I'm losing the right kind of weight and that I'm protecting the things that matter as I age."*
- **Open design question:** how does Bold actually measure this — DEXA, smart scale, measurements, labs? See [[open-questions]].

*(Source: 2026-05-05 readout, "Value Prop 2: Body Composition: Lean Mass & Bone Density." [[findings]] #6–8.)*

## 4. Older-adult-specific strength and balance plan

Exercise only works if it's clearly personalized and accessible.

- **What resonates:** seated options, balance-safe routines, strength progression, osteoporosis/bone support, joint-friendly options.
- **What to avoid:** generic-looking fitness imagery, anything that requires prolonged standing, anything that signals "this app is for younger people."

*(Source: 2026-05-05 readout, "Winning value props" #4. [[findings]] #15–16.)*

## 5. Convenient diet guidance — not another logging app

Help users decide *before* they eat, not record after.

- **What resonates:** meal planning before eating (not just logging after), food convenience (menus, prepared foods, restaurant choices, low-effort meals), non-restrictive framing (lots of users are sick of keto/low-sugar diets), photo-based food tracking only briefly to seed personalization, strength/balance/seated exercises, age-appropriate movement, a real person checking in / encouraging / helping adjust.
- **What to avoid:** leading with generic habit tracking (not differentiated), requiring wearables, generic exercise that looks inaccessible to older adults, overstating coach/oversight to the point it feels surveillance-y.

*(Source: 2026-05-05 readout, "Value Prop 3: Personalized diet and exercise planning that cuts out decision fatigue." [[findings]] #9–14.)*

## 6. Provider in the loop — for safety AND motivational accountability, not for chat

A clinician's presence is reassuring on two axes: clinical (side effects, dosage, safety) and emotional ("I'm not in it by myself" — someone who notices, adjusts, and keeps you going). It is not reassuring as ongoing chat coaching.

- **What resonates:** check-ins specifically about side effects, dose adjustment, tapering, and accountability for lifestyle changes the user is already making. Motivational framing ("someone who keeps you on track") tested better than credential framing ("doctor-supervised"). A nutritionist or coach role may carry the motivational load equally well — worth testing as a differentiator.
- **Frame the follow-up loop as "held accountable for real change," not "care plan + follow-up appointments."** The mechanic (appointments + plan) is table stakes; the *reason it matters* — accountability — is the benefit. *(See [[findings]] #34.)*
- **What to avoid:** chatty doctor interactions, generic lifestyle advice, anything that feels like the provider is telling the user what they already know.

*(Source: 2026-05-05 readout, "Winning value props" #6. [[findings]] #5, #27.)*
